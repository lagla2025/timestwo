## DEEP BENCH Q&A (back pocket, don't say unless asked)

*The distilled 12-card Q&A is in QA_Final.txt. Below is the full depth for hard follow-ups.*

**"Your research subjects are on academic licenses, not paying subscribers. Why would paying users behave the same way?"**

We tested with people who engage with the content daily. Paying subscribers engage more deeply because they chose to be there. The behaviour is the same. The commitment level is higher for paying users, not lower. The academic license gave us access to the behaviour. The paywall is what makes the pool valuable. One proves the other.

**"Who are your competitors?"**

Hinge, Bumble, Tinder: performed identity, broken business model. Raya: celebrity-gated. The League: LinkedIn-verified, career filter. Facebook Dating: 3 billion users, all the data, nobody talks about it. Data without trust is noise. Spotify on Hinge: decorative, still self-presentation. Guardian Soulmates ran for 16 years, 500K members, 15M pounds revenue. Right audience. Wrong mechanism. Killed by free apps. Nobody is using publisher behavioural data as the matching engine. That is the white space.

**"What's stopping Hinge or Bumble from just doing what you do?"**

Their revenue model depends on the mechanism that is damaging their users. Intermittent reinforcement. Decision fatigue. Performance anxiety. They are throwing AI at it. But AI applied to a broken mechanism is still a broken mechanism. You cannot algorithmically fix a product whose revenue depends on the problem it is supposed to solve. Times Two sidesteps the entire trap. The matching happens inside an ecosystem people already trust and already pay for.

**"Has this been tried before? What is the precedent?"**

Guardian Soulmates. This is the direct comp for everything we are doing.

Soulmates launched in 2004 as a commissioned product. Kate Morgan-Locke, Head of Enterprise at Guardian Newspapers, built it as a "true extension of the newspapers." Design and editorial were handled internally. Technical infrastructure was provided by Telecom Express and Persuasion Lab. The Guardian provided the audience and the brand. Soulmates launched with zero users. Every one of those 500,000 members came from the Guardian's existing readership. 80% joined specifically because of the Guardian brand. In 2009, The Dating Lab was brought in to provide the mobile backbone. Later, the Guardian brought everything in-house. Costs rose. Profitability fell. They closed in June 2020.

It failed because it was still profile-based. Same mechanism as Tinder. When Tinder launched at zero cost, paying 32 pounds for the same mechanic became indefensible. There was also a data breach in 2017. Third party. Guardian brand. Trust damage that a publisher cannot easily recover from. Times Two owns the trust and safety stack entirely. That liability never touches the publisher.

Times Two uses the data Soulmates never touched. The behavioural signals. What people actually read, play, and engage with. That is not iteration. That is a different product. And the licensing model is the same: we build the engine, the publisher keeps the brand. The precedent exists.

**"What about Facebook Dating?"**

Free. More personal data than any platform on earth. Nobody talks about it. Data without trust is noise. Nobody wants their romantic life adjacent to their aunt's holiday photos. Times Two sits inside a publisher ecosystem people already trust and already pay for.

**"What about the SWOT?"**

Strengths: first mover in publisher-embedded dating, behavioural data nobody else has, built-in trust from publisher brand, direct precedent in Soulmates white-label licensing model. Weaknesses: solo founder, no technical co-founder yet, dependent on publisher partnerships, no users until a publisher says yes. Opportunities: market in decline with no credible replacement, publishers actively seeking new revenue lines, Crossplay proves willingness. Threats: publisher says no or takes too long, privacy regulation tightens, users find content signals creepy.

**"How does the AI/Wingman work technically?"**

API customer. We call the model, pass the data, get the brief, display it. Anthropic or OpenAI never sees who the user is. API costs are per call, small, scale with usage. Standard SaaS unit economics. We are building a product layer on top of infrastructure that already exists and gets better every year. Our moat is the data pipeline, the system prompt, the product logic, and the match algorithm. None of that lives at the AI provider.

**"What if NYT says no?"**

NYT is an accelerant, not a prerequisite. If NYT says not yet, we start with a willing publisher and prove it there first. This is a sequencing story, not a single point of failure. The Guardian is the natural first partner: Soulmates precedent, experimental culture, no paywall barrier.

**"Why not just partner with one publisher?"**

One publisher is enough to launch. The moat is multi-publisher signal aggregation over time. No single publisher can replicate what NYT plus FT plus Guardian plus The Atlantic build together. First publisher proves the model. Second proves the network effect. The six-publisher number is the destination, not the prerequisite.

**"Walk me through the Year 3 revenue."**

20,000 users. 30% convert to paid at $8/month: $576K user subs. Publisher licensing $2-3/active user/month across all 20,000: $480-720K. Venue partnerships and events: $200-400K. Total: $1.5-2M. Conservative. Assumes ramp from one to two publishers. Does not require all six.

**"How did you arrive at a $5M pre-money valuation?"**

Comparable seed-stage B2B SaaS valuations. 20 years domain expertise. Size of addressable market. Defensibility of matching methodology. Working prototype. 16 qualitative interviews across 10 countries. Active subscriber study. $5M is the middle of $3-8M range for pre-seed/seed B2B SaaS in 2025-2026.

**"Won't further funding rounds dilute the seed investor's 20%?"**

Yes. Pre-dilution returns. Standard. Series A typically takes 20-25%, taking seed from 20% to roughly 15%. At $20M Year 3 valuation, 15% is $3M on $1.25M. Still 2.4x. Before multi-publisher aggregation kicks in.

**"How much do publishers pay for the infrastructure?"**

$2-3 per active user per month. Publisher charges end user $8-15/month. Keeps the difference. 60-75% margin on a product they did not build. Soulmates charged users 16-32 pounds/month on web, up to 12 pounds/week on app, and the publisher kept all of it. Our model gives the publisher majority margin on revenue they could not generate without us.

**"What if a publisher just acquires Times Two outright?"**

Acquisition is a valid exit. Price floor at Year 2-3 probably $15-30M. Good for investors. But selling to one publisher caps upside because they would kill multi-publisher model. NYT gets better matches when FT and Guardian data is also in the system. Buying us and locking it down makes their own product worse. Acquisition is an exit path. Licensing is the growth path.

**"How does the subscriber actually get Times Two?"**

Three packaging options, publisher chooses:

Option A (cleanest): Standalone add-on. Subscriber pays $8/month. Publisher keeps $5-6. We keep $2-3. Like adding Cooking or Athletic. No new app. Just a new tab or section.

Option B: Bundled into All Access or equivalent. No extra charge. Publisher pays us $2-3/user from existing sub. Incentive is churn reduction.

Option C: Freemium within ecosystem. Free to browse and see content overlap. Pay $3-5/month for the reveal, chat, starters, venues. Least lift from publisher. They grant API access. We handle product and billing.

Guardian likely starts Option C (lowest risk). NYT likely Option A (add-on infrastructure already exists). Flexibility is a feature, not indecision.

**"Why can't NYT just do this themselves?"**

Two reasons. First, NYT does not build new product categories from scratch. They acquire them. The Athletic for $550M. Wordle. Wirecutter. But every one of those acquisitions bought a proven product with a proven audience. We do not have traction yet. We have a thesis. Phase 1 builds the evidence that makes us worth licensing or acquiring.

Second, even if NYT built it, they would only build it for themselves. The multi-publisher model that makes the matching better is something NYT would never create because it benefits their competitors. Only an independent infrastructure company has the incentive to aggregate across publishers.

One more thing. NYT has a Not Invented Here problem. We do not walk in with millions of users. We walk in with a mechanism, evidence that it works, and a proposition: we will earn our keep inside your ecosystem. The retention numbers will prove it or they will not. We are asking them to run a pilot. If it does not reduce churn, turn it off. The downside risk to the publisher is close to zero.

**"Are you pitching two products? Dating and friendship?"**

No. We are building a dating product. That is the focus. 62% of our survey respondents want friendship, not dates. That tells us the addressable market is wider than dating alone. But we are building one product. Dating. If it works, the engine can expand to friendship the way Bumble expanded from dating to BFF. Same app. New use case. Future upside, not current scope.

**"What if only one publisher says yes?"**

Guardian alone (1.3M subs): roughly 175K eligible singles. Revenue at maturity: roughly $2M/year. Real business.

NYT alone (12.78M subs): roughly 1.7M eligible singles. Revenue at maturity: $10M plus.

First publisher proves the model. Second proves the network effect.

**"What is your Plan B if no publisher says yes in Year 1?"**

Best case: Guardian says yes. Soulmates precedent. Experimental culture.

Good case: Mid-tier publisher like The Atlantic or New Yorker. Smaller pool, rich content signals.

Worst realistic case: No publisher in Year 1. We pivot to standalone app with OAuth integrations pulling reading data with user consent. Less data, weaker signal, but functional.

Actual worst case: The thesis does not hold. We find out during Phase 1 before spending the $1.1M. The $150K is designed to answer this question.

**"What about church and state? How do you handle editorial integrity?"**

Times Two is a commercial product. It sits on the same shelf as Cooking, Games, Athletic, and Wirecutter. All commercial. None of them are journalism. The editorial side does not touch any of them.

The data we use for matching is the same behavioral data NYT already collects and monetizes commercially through their Perspective Targeting system for ad sales. They track what subscribers read, how long they spend, what they engage with. They use it to sell ads. We use it to find matches. Same data. New application. With explicit user consent.

**"What about chat safety and brand risk?"**

NYT already lets strangers write things under their brand. 12,000 moderated comments a day. The institutional comfort with moderating user-generated text at scale already exists. Chat moderation is the same muscle, different product.

The paywall is the first structural filter. Bots and trolls do not pay $25/month. If someone gets banned, re-entry costs $25/month for a new subscription, not zero for a new email. That changes behaviour fundamentally.

Times Two builds and operates all trust and safety. The publisher does not touch any of it. We own the liability. They license the product. We own the risk.

**"What about existing publisher dating sites? The Dating Lab, White Label Dating?"**

Publisher-branded dating has existed for 22 years. But every one of those products is a standalone website. Separate URL. Separate login. Separate payment. No integration with publisher content or subscriber data. The publisher logo is on the door and nothing else.

Times Two is fundamentally different. The subscription is the gate. Your behavior is real, not performed. The paywall is the filter. Same licensing model. Completely different product. White Label Dating's parent company Venntro collapsed in 2024 with 2 million pounds in debt. The old model is breaking.

**"What about pricing and freemium details?"**

NYT Cooking is 6 dollars a month standalone. NYT All Access is 25. Hinge is 7. Bumble is 5 to 10. We are modelling at 8 dollars a month as an add-on or standalone.

On freemium. The free tier shows you what you share with someone. The content overlap. But not who they are. No photo. No name. No chat. The paid tier is the reveal. The person. The conversation starters. The chat. Content first. That's the product philosophy built into the pricing.

**"What's a SaaS multiple and how does it work?"**

SaaS stands for Software as a Service. It means recurring subscription revenue, not one-off sales. Investors value SaaS companies as a multiple of their annual recurring revenue because that revenue is predictable and compounds. A company earning $2M a year in recurring revenue at a 10x multiple is valued at $20M. The multiple reflects growth rate, market size, and defensibility. 8-10x is standard for seed-stage B2B SaaS. We are using 8-10x because it is conservative and credible at our stage.
