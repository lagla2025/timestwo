## SLIDE 2 — That was the best version.

[Crisis stats on screen. Let them land.]

Match Group. 48 billion to 7 billion. Down 85%. Bumble. 8 billion to 480 million. Down 94%. 1.5 million paying users lost across both platforms. In a single year.

Finding you someone means losing you as a customer. There's no incentive to fix it.

The bottleneck is the introduction. Every app starts with who you want to be. Times Two starts with what you read, what you play, and how you show up.

## SLIDE 3 — Read. Listen. Watch. Play.

But every one of those people also subscribes to things. They read articles. They listen to podcasts. They play Wordle before they've spoken to anyone. None of that is performed. Nobody reads a 4,000-word opinion piece to impress a match. They read it because something in it mattered to them.

One of our participants said it perfectly: what you engage with every day is truly who you are.

That behaviour is sitting inside publisher subscriber data right now. Untouched. We're using it.

---

## SLIDE 4 — The Wingman.

We call it the Wingman. It watches what you engage with. When it spots a genuine overlap with someone, it introduces you. Not with a list of facts. With a brief. Three lines. With opinions.

"The Middle Manager piece. He finished it too. Neither of you knew. That doesn't happen often. Marcella Hazan's tomato sauce. Noted. Same instinct. You thinking what I'm thinking? I'm stepping in."

That's the brief. But the Wingman also has a voice. "Connections. Every day. No mistakes. The kind of discipline you aspire to have." It takes a position on whether this match is worth your time. No other app does that. And it doesn't stop at introductions. It helps you get better. "Your longest conversations start from Culture. That's your best material. Use it." No dating app in history offers a learning loop.

---

## SLIDE 5 — The flywheel.

Why would a publisher do this? Three reasons. Retention: meet someone through your NYT account and try cancelling. You won't. Gen Z: they play Games through academic licensing now. With Times Two, when that access expires, it's where something real happened. They want to stay. Revenue: this isn't a feature. It's a new product. NYT Relationships. Guardian Connect.

And every couple that meets through Times Two is a story. That story becomes content. A Modern Love essay. A 30-second video. That content brings the next couple. The flywheel never stops.

We build the engine. They keep the brand.

## SLIDE 6 — The market.

22 and a half million subscribers across premium publishers. Apply standard demographics: 3.7 million eligible singles. For context, Bumble has 3.7 million paying users globally. Same number. Ours were pre-qualified by a publisher paywall before we ever met them. At 12 dollars a month, that's a TAM of 530 million.

The model is B2B SaaS. User subscriptions, publisher licensing, venue referrals, and live events from day one. The moat is multi-publisher signal aggregation. No single publisher can replicate what NYT plus FT plus Guardian build together.

## SLIDE 7 — The ask.

We've run 16 qualitative interviews at TCD Business School. 10 countries represented. The thesis holds. NYT launched CrossPlay in January 2026. Their first multiplayer game. In-game chat built in. One million downloads in weeks. NYT is already facilitating connections among its subscribers. Dating is no longer a stretch.

60K removes the behavioural uncertainty before we ask publishers to take a risk. Guardian validates. NYT scales.

And one more thing. NYT is an accelerant, not a prerequisite. If NYT says yes, we scale faster. If they say not yet, we start with the Guardian and prove it there first. This is a sequencing story, not a single point of failure.

## Q&A NOTES (back pocket, don't say unless asked)

**"Your research subjects are on academic licenses, not paying subscribers. Why would paying users behave the same way?"**

We tested with people who engage with the content daily. Paying subscribers engage more deeply because they chose to be there. The behaviour is the same. The commitment level is higher for paying users, not lower. The academic license gave us access to the behaviour. The paywall is what makes the pool valuable. One proves the other.

**"Who are your competitors?"**

Hinge, Bumble, Tinder: performed identity, broken business model. Raya: celebrity-gated, the flex is the membership. The League: LinkedIn-verified, career filter, not content matching. Facebook Dating: 3 billion users, all their data, nobody talks about it. Data without trust is noise. Spotify on Hinge: decorative, still self-presentation. Guardian Soulmates ran for 16 years, 500K members, £15M revenue. Right audience. Wrong mechanism. Killed by free apps. Nobody is using publisher behavioural data as the matching engine. Nobody has built this. That's the white space.

**"What's stopping Hinge or Bumble from just doing what you do?"**

Hinge, Bumble, and Tinder aren't failing because they made bad decisions. They're failing because their revenue model depends on the same mechanism that's damaging their users. Intermittent reinforcement: the same slot-machine loop that keeps users swiping also makes them feel progressively worse. Decision fatigue: endless options sound like a feature but they're a bug. The paradox of choice means more options, worse decisions, less satisfaction. Performance anxiety: social media has created pressure around dating that the apps both feed and profit from. They know all of this. They're throwing AI at it. But AI applied to a broken mechanism is still a broken mechanism. You cannot algorithmically fix a product whose revenue depends on the problem it's supposed to solve. Times Two sidesteps the entire trap. The matching happens inside an ecosystem people already trust and already pay for. No slot machine. No performance pressure. No bottomless swipe stack.

**"What about the SWOT?"**

Strengths: first mover in publisher-embedded dating, behavioural data nobody else has, built-in trust from publisher brand. Weaknesses: solo founder, no technical co-founder yet, dependent on publisher partnerships. Opportunities: $4.5B market in decline with no credible replacement, publishers actively seeking new revenue lines, CrossPlay proves willingness. Threats: publisher says no, privacy regulation tightens, users find content signals creepy (kill criterion 3).

**"What about Facebook Dating? They have 3 billion users and all the data in the world."**

Facebook Dating launched in 2019. It's free. It has access to more personal data than any platform on earth. And nobody talks about it. Because data without trust is noise. Facebook's brand is a liability in dating. Nobody wants their romantic life adjacent to their aunt's holiday photos and political arguments. Times Two sits inside a publisher ecosystem people already trust and already pay for. That's the difference. Having data isn't the point. Having the right data, used the right way, inside a trusted environment is the point.

**"How does the AI/Wingman work technically?"**

Times Two is an API customer. We call the model, pass in the data, get back the brief, display it to the user. Anthropic or OpenAI never sees who the user is. They just process the request. API costs are per call, small, and scale with usage. As users grow, API costs grow but so do subscriptions. Standard SaaS unit economics. We are not building a foundational model. We are building a product layer on top of infrastructure that already exists and gets better every year as the underlying models improve. Our moat is the data pipeline from the publisher ecosystem, the system prompt that defines the Wingman voice, the product logic around when and how the Wingman speaks, and the match algorithm that decides who gets introduced. None of that lives at Anthropic or OpenAI. That is ours. "We build on top of the best available models. Our moat is the data and the product logic, not the model itself. That is where the value compounds."
