**"How does affinity become connection in the product? What's the evidence?"**

The behavioral science is established. Shared experiences in common ground create social closeness. That's Wolf and Tomasello, 2020, published in the Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. Perceived similarity drives attraction more powerfully than actual similarity. That's Byrne's similarity-attraction hypothesis from 1971, confirmed in Montoya's 2008 meta-analysis. When someone shares your attitudes, it validates your view of yourself and the world. That is inherently rewarding.

Times Two makes shared content experiences visible. Two people both finished a long read that most people abandoned. Both got Genius on Spelling Bee. Both saved the same recipe on a Friday night. That is perceived similarity made visible. The research says that is the strongest predictor of initial attraction.

The progression in the product:

Step 1. Content consumption. Passive. Authentic. Not performed. This is who they are when nobody is watching.

Step 2. Affinity signal. The Wingman detects overlap. Shared taste. Shared worldview.

Step 3. Introduction. The Wingman presents the match with a brief and a conversation starter. Not "hey." Something real. "The ending of that Middle Manager piece. Did you see it coming?"

Step 4. Real conversation. Easy, because the common ground already existed before either of them knew the other existed. This is what no profile-based app can replicate.

Step 5. Human connection. Something develops between two people. Not between a person and an app.

Step 6. Retention. The subscriber does not cancel the subscription that made this possible. Not because the platform is addictive. Because the platform is where something real happened.

Honest caveat: the research shows similarity effects are stronger in laboratory settings than in established long-term relationships. Shared content signals create the conditions for connection. They do not guarantee it. The introduction is easier. The relationship still takes work. We don't overclaim.

The structural proof is in the incentives. Hinge and Bumble make money when you keep swiping. Their revenue correlates with your loneliness. **"The white-label dating market already exists. How are you different from The Dating Lab or White Label Dating?"**

The Dating Lab has powered publisher dating for 22 years. White Label Dating powered 6,500 sites before its parent company Venntro went into administration in August 2024 with over 2 million pounds in debt. 15 million users had to migrate to HubPeople. The traditional white-label revenue-share model is broken. High customer acquisition costs plus legacy tech debt made it unsustainable.

Both models are standalone websites. Separate URL. Separate login. Separate payment. The publisher logo is on the door and nothing else. No subscriber requirement. No content integration. No behavioural data. The brand is a trust signal and nothing more.

Times Two is embedded in the subscription ecosystem. You must be a subscriber. Your content behaviour is the matching signal. The paywall is the filter. Same licensing model as The Dating Lab. Completely different product. They proved publishers will license dating technology. We build what that technology should have been.

**"PURE is growing at 46% while Tinder is down 14%. Why wouldn't someone just build another PURE?"**

PURE succeeds because it has a clear identity and privacy-first architecture. It serves a specific community mass-market apps ignore. That is exactly the Times Two thesis. The difference is the matching signal. PURE still uses profiles. Still self-presentation. Still performed identity. Times Two uses behavioural data nobody performs. PURE proves niche community-centric products win right now. Times Two is a niche community-centric product with a structurally superior matching engine. PURE is the proof of concept for our market positioning. Not our competitor.

**"Won't NYT editorial resist this? What about church and state? More specifically."**

The wall is already down. NYT proved it themselves. Cooking. Games. The Athletic for 550 million dollars. Wirecutter. T Brand Studio. None of those are journalism. All carry the NYT brand. The institutional comfort with commercial lifestyle verticals has never been higher. Times Two is the next vertical in a sequence NYT already started. Editorial does not touch any of it. Same separation as Cooking and Games.

The UK never had this problem. British publishers integrated dating, puzzles, and personals with news for over a century. Joan Ball ran the world's first computer dating service in London in 1964. The US is catching up. NYT is leading that shift.

What we call the paywall as a filter, UK market analysts call newspaper snobbiness. Same concept. Different vocabulary. Readers identify as "Times types" or "Telegraph readers." The publication is a proxy for social and intellectual compatibility. That self-selection has powered publisher dating for 22 years. Times Two harnesses it with data instead of profiles.

**"What about the data? Church and state more specifically. NYT Perspective Targeting, data access levels."**

NYT already tracks subscriber behavior at a granular level. They built a proprietary first-party data system called Perspective Targeting that maps 42 emotions and 10 motivations against reader behavior. They use this commercially for ad targeting. Digital ad revenue was 98 million dollars in Q3 2025 alone. They do not use Google AdSense. They built their own system. The data we need for matching is a subset of what they already collect and already monetize. We are proposing a new application of existing commercial data with explicit user consent.

Data access spectrum: Level 1 (launch) is games, cooking, and section-level engagement. Standard API. Level 2 is completion rates and browse depth. Requires publisher partnership integration. Level 3 is article-level signals. May never be needed. The product works at Level 1. Each level up makes matching better.

**"What about content moderation at scale?"**

NYT already moderates 12,000 user-generated comments a day using a hybrid AI and human system built with Alphabet's Jigsaw division. The AI was trained on 16 million hand-moderated comments over 10 years. They went from 10% of articles open for comments to 80% using this system. The institutional comfort with moderating user-generated text at scale already exists at NYT. Times Two chat moderation uses the same approach. AI flags, humans review edge cases.

**"What about trust and safety in detail?"**

The paywall is the first line of defense. Bots and trolls do not pay 25 dollars a month to harass people. If someone gets banned, they cannot just create a new account with a different email. They need a different publisher subscription. The cost of re-entry after a ban is 25 dollars, not zero. That changes behaviour.

We build and operate all trust and safety. AI message scanning before delivery. One-tap reporting. Graduated enforcement. The publisher licenses the product. We own the liability.

**"What about pricing and freemium details?"**

On pricing. NYT Cooking is 6 dollars a month standalone. NYT All Access is 25. Hinge is 7. Bumble is 5 to 10. We are modelling at 8 dollars a month as an add-on or standalone. Competitive with dating apps and below NYT's own pricing for Cooking.

On freemium. The product is free to start. The Wingman watches. You get matched. You can chat. The paid tier unlocks the full experience. We don't gate the number of matches. We gate the depth of the experience. The timing for conversion depends on the publisher partner and the market. We are flexible by design.

US and UK simultaneously. That's where 85% of premium English-language publisher subscribers live. Ireland is our proof of concept through the TCD connection. Australia and Canada follow in Year 3.

**"What about the target demographic in more detail?"**

NYT's median subscriber age is 43. 62% are under 50. The Guardian is similar at 44. FT averages 49. Economist skews older at 61. The 18 to 29 cohort exists but they're largely on academic licenses, not paying. Our real market is paying subscribers aged 30 and up.

The 55 plus cohort adds another 1.5 million singles. This group is growing and completely underserved by every dating app on the market. The 25 to 34 cohort is smaller in publisher subscriptions today but it grows every year as digital natives age into the platform. The audience compounds year over year.
